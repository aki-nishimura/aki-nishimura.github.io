Monte Carlo Methods for Bayesian Computation  Statistical inference under Bayesian paradigm typically rely on MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo) to generate samples from posterior distributions. HMC (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) is one of the best existing general-purpose algorithms and forms a backbone of popular probabilistic programming languages Stan and PyMC. [caption id="attachment_421" align="alignright" width="190"] Watch HMC in action here[/caption] HMC is not fail-proof, however, and I have been working to address some of the most prominent short-comings of HMC. My work discontinuous HMC provides a solution to arguably the most prominent drawback of HMC: its inability to handle discrete parameters. This is achieved by embedding discrete parameters into a continuous space and simulating Hamiltonian dynamics on a piecewise smooth density function. Another work geometrically tempered HMC improves HMC's performance on multi-modal distributions using differential geometric technique, whose application to MCMC was pioneered by Girolamiand Calderhead (2011). I have also developed a generalized acceptance-rejection scheme to allow for the use of adaptive numerical solvers in HMC and related algorithms. I am actively investigating further extensions of HMC as well as other MCMC algorithms, including the ones based on (stochastic) approximations of the target density and its gradients. CATHGEN Project: role of genetics in cardiovascular deceases

I have been providing statistical analysis for the Duke CATHGEN Research Project, whose mission is to advance our understanding of the roles genes play in coronary heart disease: the leading cause of death worldwide. In collaboration with cardiovascular geneticists at Duke School of Medicine and air-pollution epidemiologists at EPA, we hope to identify genetic mutations (SNPs, to be more precise) that make individuals more susceptible to the detrimental effects of air pollution. This is an ambitious goal given the large number of patients and genetic mutations involved in this study: 9232 patients and approximately 700,000 SNPs. The presence of potential confounding factors also make it challenging to separate true signals from noise. As a first step, I have demonstrated the association between a cardiovascular health outcome and a long-term exposure to ground-level ozone using a functional regression model (see below) with a time-varying baseline to control for potential confounding. I will be posting more as we make progress toward our goal.
